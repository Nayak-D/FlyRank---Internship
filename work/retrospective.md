# Final Retrospective

In Week 1, I set out to build a practical machine-learning project around search and discoverability. The initial idea was broad: find a useful pattern in content data and turn it into something a team could act on. The work became more specific when I chose the Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring lane. Instead of trying to explain or predict Google's ranking system, I framed a narrower decision: when a content team has more pages to inspect than time allows, which pages should a strategist review first?

That framing changed the project. The output stopped being an abstract model score and became a ranked review queue. I built a transparent baseline using visibility, freshness risk, and engagement risk, then compared it with a class-balanced Random Forest using observable page-level signals. The final feature set included impressions, position, content age, clicks, CTR, scroll rate, sessions, and content shape. I kept client identifiers only for grouped validation and excluded target-derived decision flags from the model features.

The biggest change in my thinking was learning to treat evaluation design as part of the product. A random row split would have made the result easier to produce but less trustworthy because pages from the same client portfolio could appear on both sides of the evaluation. The client-grouped holdout made the comparison more demanding and more relevant to the generalization question. On that split, the Random Forest reached ROC AUC 0.750 and Precision@50 0.740, compared with 0.627 and 0.240 for the baseline. I describe that as an observed 3.08x lift on this dataset and split, not as a causal claim or a forecast of future page performance.

The public paper and portfolio also changed how I think about completion. A notebook alone can contain good analysis, but a stranger needs a clear path from the business problem to the evidence. I wrote the paper to connect the FlyRank content problem, the data safety choices, the method, the comparison, the limitations, and the editorial recommendations. I also hardened the portfolio's contact flow and documented the places where deployment, Lighthouse, physical-device testing, and form-delivery proof still need more evidence.

The next version I would build is a genuinely time-aware forecasting workflow. I would use features from a past observation window and define the target from a later window, then compare a grouped and time-aware validation design. I would also add calibrated confidence and a stronger thin-signal policy so low-traffic pages are not presented with false precision. Finally, I would connect the review queue to a small human feedback loop: record what reviewers chose to refresh, what changed afterward, and which recommendations were useful.

Three lessons transfer beyond this project. First, frame the decision before choosing the model; a useful ranking problem is easier to validate than a vague prediction goal. Second, make the baseline and split visible; the comparison is more persuasive when a reviewer can see what the model had to beat and why the evaluation is honest. Third, treat limitations and deployment evidence as part of the deliverable, not an appendix to hide. Naming the proxy label, thin-signal cases, stale deployment risk, and missing external evidence made the work more credible and gave me a concrete next-build list.

I used AI as a coding and writing partner for notebook structure, documentation drafts, validation commands, and wording reviews. I checked the claims against the repository outputs, ran the executable notebook cells, inspected the public pages, and kept the final limitations and deployment statements grounded in what I could verify myself.
