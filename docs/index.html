Abstract

FlyRank content teams need a practical way to decide which pages deserve attention when a large portfolio contains more possible refreshes than a reviewer can inspect in one cycle. This capstone asks: which pages should a content strategist check first? It builds a ranked scoring system that combines a hand-written baseline rule with a Random Forest classifier trained on observable, pre-decision signals.

Evaluated on a client-holdout split, the model reached Precision@50 = 0.740 versus the baseline rule's 0.240. That is a 3.08x improvement in placing genuinely declining pages near the top of the review queue on this dataset and split.

The result is directional decision support, not a causal claim about refreshing content or predicting Google's ranking algorithm. The ranking is designed to help a human reviewer decide what to inspect, refresh, expand, or leave alone.

Introduction / Problem statement

FlyRank's content problem is operational as much as technical: a strategist may have thousands of pages to review, but only enough time to inspect a small queue each cycle. A website can therefore contain pages that are quietly losing visibility without a clear, repeatable way to decide where human attention should start.

The real decision this work supports is: given limited reviewer capacity, which pages should a content strategist check first?

The action this ranking feeds is direct: a reviewer opens the top-ranked pages and decides whether to refresh, expand, or leave them. Getting the ranking wrong has a cost either way: reviewer time is wasted on pages that were never declining, or a genuinely declining high-traffic page is missed.

The project therefore treats ranking quality and interpretability as more important than an impressive but opaque score. Every result is framed for a human-in-the-loop editorial workflow.

Data

This work uses the FlyRank ML Internship starter release, a public-safe extract of 30,000 content-page rows. It contains page-level visibility, engagement, freshness, and content-shape signals. No client names, domains, titles, keywords, or private queries appear in the paper.

  • Rows scored: 30,000
  • Target: is_declining_label
  • Declining-label rate: 0.542
  • Validation: client-grouped holdout

The label is a current-window proxy for observed declining behavior, not a genuine future outcome. That distinction is a first-class limitation: this paper reports directional ranking performance, not a validated forecast of what will happen after a page refresh.

Leakage guardrail: target-derived decision flags were excluded from modeling. Pseudonymous IDs were used only to create grouped validation splits, never as predictive content features.

Methodology

Signal verification before modeling

Candidate signals were checked in buckets before entering the baseline or learned model. The audit kept the final claim narrow: a signal could inform review prioritization without being treated as proof of causality.

Signal Verdict Finding
Days since update vs. decline rate MIXED Staleness rises across part of the window but breaks down in the oldest, smaller bucket.
CTR and visibility risk CONFIRMED Visible pages with weak engagement create a more actionable review signal than age alone.

Baseline rule

The hand-written baseline combines visibility, freshness, and underperformance into a transparent review score. It creates a ranked queue with explicit action reasons rather than a hidden probability.

score = visibility × freshness risk × engagement risk

Model and features

The selected model is a class-balanced Random Forest with 300 trees and maximum depth 8. It can combine multiple signals and capture non-linear interactions that a fixed rule cannot, while remaining suitable for a reviewer-facing ranking workflow.

  • days_with_impressions
  • log_impressions_90d
  • avg_position
  • content_age_days
  • char_count
  • word_count
  • log_clicks_90d
  • ctr
  • scroll_rate
  • days_with_sessions

Validation design

Evaluation uses a client-grouped holdout rather than a random row split. This prevents pages from the same client portfolio appearing across both training and test data, which would make generalization look stronger than it is.

The work does not claim to predict Google's algorithm. It provides a defendable ranking of editorial review opportunities based on measured search behavior.

What drove the ranking

days_with_impressions, log_impressions_90d, avg_position, and content_age_days were the strongest recorded signals in the exported model report. The queue therefore prioritizes pages with measurable visibility context instead of treating every old page as equally urgent.

Error interpretation

The model's review queue is strongest when pages have enough visibility and engagement context to support a meaningful comparison. It is less reliable for thin-signal pages, especially very low-traffic pages with zero or near-zero engagement. That is a routing concern for editorial operations, not a reason to hide the error pattern.

Limitations & honest framing

  • The label is a current-window proxy, not an observed future outcome. This is a directional signal, not a validated forecast.
  • The 3.08x improvement is observed on this dataset and this client-holdout split. It is not a causal claim about what refreshing a page would do.
  • The system does not reflect or attempt to reverse-engineer Google's ranking algorithm.
  • Thin-signal pages remain the hardest cases because low traffic and low engagement provide little evidence either way.
  • The safest production use is a ranked queue for human review, not an autonomous publishing decision.

Ranked recommendations

  1. Use the model ranking for weekly review-queue generation. The observed Precision@50 lift is meaningful on the held-out client groups and gives reviewers a stronger first pass than the hand-written rule.
  2. Keep a separate thin-signal track. Route very low-traffic or zero-engagement pages to lighter-touch review instead of forcing a confident ranking where the evidence is weak.
  3. Inspect reason codes before editing. The queue is an ordering mechanism; the final refresh, expand, or monitor decision still belongs to a content strategist.
  4. Replace the proxy label next. A future version should train on past-window features and a next-window outcome so the system can be evaluated as a real forecasting workflow.
